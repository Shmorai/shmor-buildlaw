Construction adjudication timetable: what happens in 28 days

Adjudication is the fast-track route for construction disputes in England and Wales. The statutory framework gives a tight timetable: a referral within 7 days of the notice and a decision within 28 days of referral. Here is how those days actually run, and where parties lose time or rights.

Where the 28 days come from

Section 108 of the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 gives parties to a qualifying construction contract the right to refer a dispute to adjudication at any time. The contract must provide for the adjudicator to be appointed and the dispute referred within 7 days of the notice, and for a decision within 28 days of referral (or longer if the parties agree after referral). The referring party can agree to a 14-day extension. If a contract does not comply, the Scheme for Construction Contracts fills the gap. JCT, NEC and most trade-body contracts have their own compliant rules, so always read yours. See our adjudication guide for the basics.

Step by step: notice to decision

  1. Notice of adjudication (day 0). Under the Scheme this is written, goes to every other party and briefly describes the dispute, where and when it arose, the redress sought and the parties' details. Scope matters: the adjudicator can only decide the dispute that was notified, so draft it carefully.
  2. Appointing the adjudicator. Contracts usually name a person or a nominating body. Under the Scheme the person asked must say within 2 days whether they will act, and a nominating body must make its selection within 5 days.
  3. Referral notice (within 7 days of the notice). The referring party sends the adjudicator, and copies to the other side, its full case with the contract extracts and evidence. The adjudicator's 28 days run from receipt of this document.
  4. Response. The adjudicator sets the timetable. Responding parties typically get a fraction of the 28 days, so start gathering evidence before you are served.
  5. Reply, meetings and questions. The adjudicator can ask for documents, hold a meeting or inspect the site, and may take the initiative on the facts and law.
  6. Decision (day 28, or day 42 with consent). Copies go to each party as soon as possible.

Parties can agree a longer decision period at any point after referral, which is common in complex disputes.

What if the adjudicator is late or you ignore the process?

Under the Scheme, if the decision deadline is missed, any party can serve a fresh notice and ask for a new adjudicator. If a party fails to comply with the adjudicator's directions without good reason, the adjudicator can carry on without it, draw reasonable inferences and decide on the information available. Ignoring a notice rarely makes the dispute disappear; it usually means the decision is made on the other side's evidence. Also note that a decision on a dispute that was never properly referred, or outside the contract, may be challenged on jurisdiction, but courts expect parties to comply first and argue later. Fees, costs and getting the decision enforced

Under the Scheme the adjudicator may claim reasonable fees and expenses, may decide how they are split between the parties, and the parties are jointly and severally liable to pay them. Each side usually bears its own legal costs. Under section 108A of the 1996 Act, a clause allocating adjudication costs between the parties is ineffective unless it gives the adjudicator power to allocate his fees and expenses, or was agreed in writing after the notice of adjudication, so check what your contract says.

The decision is binding until the dispute is finally decided by court, arbitration (if applicable) or agreement, and must normally be complied with straight away. Clerical errors can be corrected within 5 days of delivery. If the loser does not pay, the winner can ask the Technology and Construction Court to enforce, usually by summary judgment. Where the dispute began with a missed payment notice, see smash-and-grab adjudication.

Practical tips for working inside 28 days

  • Prepare before you serve. The referring party controls timing. Have the contract, applications, notices, valuations and correspondence organised before the notice goes out.
  • Check the payment trail first. Many disputes turn on whether valid payment and pay less notices were served.
  • Respond early. Do not wait for the referral to start building your response.
  • Consider mediation in parallel. Settlement talks do not stop the clock unless the parties agree; see construction mediation.
  • Keep it proportionate. Adjudication suits focused disputes. Big, document-heavy claims strain a 28-day window.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a construction adjudication take?
Usually about five weeks from notice: up to 7 days to refer, then 28 days for the decision, or 42 if the referring party agrees to extend. The parties can also agree a longer period after referral.
Can I start adjudication at any time?
Yes. Under the 1996 Act a party to a qualifying construction contract can give notice of adjudication at any time, including after the project has finished.
Is an adjudicator's decision final?
It is binding straight away but only temporary: it stands until the dispute is finally determined by court or arbitration, or by agreement. In practice many decisions are never reopened.
Who pays the adjudicator?
Under the Scheme both parties are jointly and severally liable for the adjudicator's reasonable fees, and the adjudicator can decide how they are split. Your contract may say something different.
