What is practical completion?

Practical completion is one of the most important dates on a project, yet most contracts don’t define it. Here’s what the courts say it means, and why it matters to both sides.

By the BuildLaw editorial team · 7 October 2026 · 1 min read

Photo: Josue Isai Ramos Figueroa on Unsplash

The legal test

In Mears Ltd v Costplan Services [2019] the Court of Appeal summarised the position: practical completion is reached when the works are complete and free from patent defects, other than ones to be ignored as trifling. Whether a defect is trifling is a question of fact and degree, judged against whether the building can be used as intended. Latent defects, which nobody knows about yet, can’t prevent it. Unfinished work and defective work are treated alike.

What practical completion triggers

  • Release of half the retention (see retentions).
  • Liquidated damages stop accruing (see liquidated damages).
  • The rectification period starts.
  • Insurance and security often pass to the client.
  • Final account and time limits for some claims start to run.

Practical tips

  • Contractors: agree a snagging process and target date early; give notice when you consider practical completion achieved.
  • Clients and administrators: certify when the test is met, not when everything is perfect; list outstanding items; don’t withhold the certificate for leverage.
  • Everyone: record the building’s condition at handover with photos.

Frequently asked questions

Can the client move in before practical completion?
Under many contracts, the client can take partial possession of part of the works with the contractor’s consent, which has its own consequences for damages, retention and insurance. Check the contract.
