Liquidated damages in construction contracts

Liquidated damages (LDs or LADs) are the pre-agreed sum a contractor pays for finishing late. They’re simple in principle, but the details decide whether they can be deducted at all.

By the BuildLaw editorial team · 9 October 2026 · 1 min read

Photo: Frames For Your Heart on Unsplash

On this page

  1. How they work
  2. Before deducting: the procedure
  3. Are LDs a penalty?
  4. Caps and termination

How they work

The contract sets a rate, for example per week of delay, and the client can recover it for each period the works are late beyond the completion date (as extended), without proving its actual loss. LDs usually replace the client’s right to general damages for delay, so the rate also limits the contractor’s exposure. Read more in our key contract terms guide.

Before deducting: the procedure

  • JCT: deduction usually depends on a non-completion certificate and the employer’s notices, given in time, plus a valid pay less notice where LDs are deducted from a payment.
  • Extensions of time: any extension of time must be decided properly first. Without a working EOT mechanism for client delays, the “prevention principle” can defeat the LD clause.
  • Rate filled in: stating “£nil” can mean no damages for delay at all, so take care with the Contract Particulars.

Are LDs a penalty?

Rarely. Since Cavendish Square Holding v Makdessi [2015], a clause is an unenforceable penalty only if it imposes a detriment out of all proportion to the innocent party’s legitimate interest. Commercial parties who negotiated a rate will usually be held to it.

Caps and termination

Many contracts cap LDs, often as a percentage of the contract sum. In Triple Point Technology v PTT [2021], the Supreme Court held that LDs accrue up to termination even if the contractor never completes; after termination, the client claims general damages. See our case studies.

Frequently asked questions

Can liquidated damages be deducted after practical completion?
Liquidated damages stop accruing at practical completion, but accrued sums can be deducted from later payments if the contract procedure and notices are followed.
