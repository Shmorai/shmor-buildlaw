Types of construction contract and procurement route

Before you choose a contract, choose how the project will be procured: who designs, who carries the risk and how the price is set. This guide compares the main UK routes and the standard forms that go with them.

Why the procurement route comes first

Every building project answers the same questions: who designs it, who builds it, who carries the risk of things going wrong, and how the price is worked out. The answers make up the procurement route, and they decide which standard form contract fits. Picking a contract first and forcing the project into it is a common cause of disputes.

How the price is set

  • Lump sum (fixed price): a single price for defined work, adjusted only for variations and other events the contract allows. Gives the client price certainty if the design is complete.
  • Measure and value (remeasurement): work is measured as it is done and valued at agreed rates, often from bills of quantities or a schedule of rates. Useful where quantities are uncertain.
  • Target cost: the contractor is paid its actual cost plus a fee, and any saving or overrun against a target is shared in agreed proportions (for example NEC4 ECC Options C and D).
  • Cost reimbursable (cost plus): actual cost plus a fee. Flexible, but the client carries most of the cost risk (for example JCT Prime Cost, NEC4 ECC Option E).

Standard forms or a bespoke contract?

Most UK projects use a standard form, usually JCT or NEC, often with a schedule of amendments. Standard forms are widely understood, comply with the Construction Act, and have been interpreted by the courts many times, which makes outcomes more predictable. Heavily amended or bespoke contracts can shift risk in ways that aren’t obvious, so read any amendments line by line.

Other forms you may meet: RIBA building contracts (domestic and concise) for smaller projects designed by an architect; the FMB plain-English contracts used by some builders; FIDIC on international work; and PPC2000 and TAC-1 for partnering. In Scotland, JCT forms are published in adapted versions by the Scottish Building Contract Committee (SBCC).

Letters of intent: a common trap

A letter of intent lets work start before the contract is signed. It can be useful, but problems arise when the formal contract is never signed: the parties can end up arguing over which terms apply, or whether only a reasonable sum is payable for work done. If you must use one, give it a cap on spend, a clear scope and an end date, and push to sign the full contract quickly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common building contract in the UK?
JCT standard forms are the most widely used on private-sector building work, while NEC4 is widely used on public-sector and infrastructure projects. The right one depends on the project, not on popularity.
Does a building contract have to be in writing?
No, a contract can be oral or partly written. But since 2011 the Construction Act’s payment and adjudication rules apply to oral and partly written construction contracts too, and a written contract avoids most arguments about what was agreed.
What’s the difference between design and build and traditional?
In a traditional contract the client’s team designs and the contractor builds what it’s told. In design and build, the contractor takes on design responsibility too, so the client deals with one party, but has less control over design details once the contract is let.
